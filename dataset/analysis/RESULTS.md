# Results summary

Written 2026-10-29 from the result files in this folder. Lexicon v1.2 (signed off 2026-10-27), **full-text scoring** (decision of 2026-10-29, see `DECISIONS.md`), rarefied entropy at K = 5 (1,000 repeats, seed 42), 95% cluster-bootstrap intervals (`question_id` for chatbots, `outlet` for news). Difference = chatbot minus news, in mean rarefied entropy (the maximum at K = 5 is 0.898). Produced by `05_rarefied_entropy.ipynb` and `06_style_vs_framing.ipynb`.

## Hypotheses

- **H1, partly supported and model-dependent.** Main result: **Llama −0.004 [−0.035, +0.027]**, **Qwen +0.058 [+0.037, +0.078]**, **pooled chatbots +0.027 [+0.002, +0.051]**. Across 14 robustness runs (42 model-by-run rows, `h1_robustness_summary.csv`) Qwen's interval excludes zero with chatbot higher in 13 of 14 (the exception is the pooled-counts run), the pooled chatbots in 9 of 14 (the interval includes zero at K = 8, with the maximum-entropy fill, without the governance frame, without ambiguous terms, and in pooled counts), and Llama in only 2 of 14 (the prompt-echo run on chatbots only, and the fixed 300-word cut). The pooled-counts run, which sums hits over a whole group, shows no difference for any comparison.
- **H2, not supported.** No chatbot-minus-news gap contrast between prompt wordings excludes zero (pooled: leaning_for − neutral −0.007 [−0.037, +0.020], leaning_against − neutral +0.005 [−0.015, +0.025]); Qwen's gap is flat across wordings (+0.059 / +0.057 / +0.057) and Llama has no gap to shrink. With 18 questions this is a failure to find an effect, not evidence of none (`h2_gap_and_contrasts.csv`).
- **H3, exploratory, no region reliably closer.** The UK is nearest to both chatbots by point estimate (JSD 0.014 pooled) and US and Other furthest (about 0.030), but every paired region difference has an interval that includes zero, and the chatbots' own split-half noise floor (0.021–0.051) is comparable to or larger than most divergences. EU (82 articles) and UK (101) are the weakest comparisons (`h3_jsd_chatbot_vs_news_regions.csv`, `h3_region_differences.csv`).
- **Style versus framing.** Style alone separates chatbot from news almost perfectly (AUC 1.00 with outlets, questions or a whole chatbot held out), the six-frame mix alone reaches 0.70–0.74 including on an unseen chatbot (so it is not a pure style artefact), and the style-matched entropy gaps repeat the main result (Llama −0.013 [−0.047, +0.020], Qwen +0.057 [+0.033, +0.077]); formatting (paragraphs, digits) cannot be matched, so style is not fully controlled (`style_vs_framing_classifiers.csv`, `style_matched_entropy_comparison.csv`).

## Composition: which frames differ (exploratory)

- **Chatbots put more of their hits in innovation/opportunity (+0.069 pooled) and governance (+0.060 pooled; Qwen +0.087, Llama +0.034 not distinguishable) and less in economy/labour (−0.114); risk/safety, rights/privacy and misinformation do not differ.** All intervals as chatbot minus all news, mean share of a text's hits; see `composition_by_frame.csv`, `composition_differences_vs_news.csv` and `composition_by_frame.png`. Exploratory: 12 comparisons, no correction, shares of one text sum to 1.

## Caveats that apply to the composition finding (and to governance in H1)

1. **Governance is partly present by design in the prompts.** Every prompt asks what governments should do about AI, 3 of the 18 questions are about governance, and the governance lexicon's words (regulator, oversight, standard, ...) occur in the prompts. A high governance share in chatbot answers is partly the question echoed back; the prompt-echo runs in `h1_lexicon_variants.csv` show how much H1 depends on it.
2. **The on-topic filter shares words with the governance lexicon.** The corpus was built by keeping articles with at least 2 regulation words (regulat*, legislat*, oversight, governance, lawmaker, ...). 9 of the 39 governance terms contain such words and supply 34% of news governance hits (24% of chatbot hits), so part of the news governance share is built in by selection. **Effect, checked:** with those 9 terms removed from both groups (`composition_governance_effect.csv`) the news governance share falls from 0.227 to 0.161 and the pooled chatbot share from 0.288 to 0.240, and the chatbot-minus-news governance difference **grows** from +0.060 [+0.006, +0.114] to **+0.079 [+0.032, +0.122]** (Llama +0.034 → +0.065 [+0.010, +0.119], now excluding zero; Qwen +0.087 → +0.093). So the overlap partly masks the difference, it does not create it. The first caveat is unaffected, and the 9 terms are the core of the frame (regulator, oversight), so the reduced score measures a narrower thing.

## Human validation: status (updated 2026-10-31)

**The dictionary agrees only moderately with the human frame labels on excerpts, and the two stance labellers agreed only weakly before they settled the disputed texts together.** Both validation tasks now have labels; for frames there is only one labelled sheet.

- **Frame validation (dictionary against human labels), 2026-10-31.** One labelled sheet (`data/validation_sheet_labelled.csv`; it is the only labelled frame sheet, so there is no human-against-human reliability for frames); 2 of the 50 texts were skipped (1 chatbot, 1 news), leaving 48 scored (24 chatbot, 24 news). The human label is the reference and the dictionary's label is computed on the same excerpt. **Overall agreement / kappa on all labels: 0.625 / 0.542; chatbot 0.708 / 0.638; news 0.542 / 0.419; the 22 news windows that pass the on-topic rule 0.545 / 0.423** (`validation_overall_by_scope.csv`, `validation_summary.csv`; confusion matrices in `validation_confusion.csv` and `validation_confusion_by_scope.csv`). **Frames below the 0.6 bar** (one-versus-rest kappa, `validation_per_frame_by_scope.csv`, with the number of texts the human / the dictionary put in the frame): all scored texts: regulation_governance 0.308 (8 human / 14 dictionary), economy_labour 0.576 (9 human / 12 dictionary); chatbot texts: regulation_governance 0.276 (5 human / 8 dictionary); news texts: innovation_opportunity 0.000 (0 human / 3 dictionary), risk_safety 0.482 (11 human / 7 dictionary), regulation_governance 0.333 (3 human / 6 dictionary), economy_labour 0.500 (4 human / 6 dictionary). At or above the bar: all texts innovation_opportunity 0.735 (5 human / 8 dictionary), risk_safety 0.670 (14 human / 10 dictionary), rights_privacy 0.636 (6 human / 3 dictionary), misinformation_integrity 1.000 (1 human / 1 dictionary); chatbot innovation_opportunity 1.000 (5 human / 5 dictionary), risk_safety 1.000 (3 human / 3 dictionary), rights_privacy 0.625 (4 human / 2 dictionary), economy_labour 0.647 (5 human / 6 dictionary); news rights_privacy 0.647 (2 human / 1 dictionary), misinformation_integrity 1.000 (1 human / 1 dictionary). Undefined: misinformation_integrity (absent from both) in the chatbot texts. The `none` label is used by the human for 5 texts and by the dictionary for 0 (it says none only when a text has no hit). The dictionary puts 14 texts in regulation_governance against 8 by the human (precision 0.357, recall 0.625). Most frequent disagreements (human → dictionary): risk_safety → regulation_governance (4), regulation_governance → economy_labour (3), rights_privacy → regulation_governance (2), none → innovation_opportunity (2). **Caveats:** one labelled sheet and no second labeller; 48 texts and 0 to 14 human labels per frame, so each per-frame kappa is unstable (one text moves it a lot) and the news-only frames rest on 0 to 11 texts; the excerpts are not the full texts the main analysis scores. The lexicon is unchanged.
- **Stance, human against human (20 shared texts): weak agreement.** The two labellers worked independently and these figures are from before any adjudication. Observed agreement 0.45 (9 of 20), Cohen's kappa **0.254** on the four labels (fair) and **-0.158** on the three plan labels (n = 11 texts that both gave for, against or mixed); both are below 0.6 (`stance_agreement.csv`, `stance_agreement_confusion.csv`). Chatbot texts: agreement 0.30, kappa -0.148; news texts: agreement 0.60, kappa 0.130 (10 texts each, so these are indications only; `stance_agreement_by_source.csv`). The disagreement is systematic: labeller A gave *mixed* to 9 of the 20 shared texts and labeller B to 2; in 8 of the 11 disputed texts A said mixed (B said for in 6 of them). **All 11 disputed texts were later settled by the two labellers together** (adjudication below); that does not change these figures, which describe the two labellers working independently.
- **Flagging check: chatbot stance labels against the prompt wording (a flagging check, not a label source).** On leaning prompts labeller A's label equals the prompt direction for 9 of 16 texts (56%), is the opposite for 3 (19%) and is mixed or none for 4 (25%); labeller B's equals it for 10 of 10 (100%). On the 10 chatbot texts both labelled (6 on leaning prompts): A 2 matches, 2 contradictions, 2 other; B 6 matches. The check flags 7 texts for a second look: 3 contradictions (S012 (A and B), S029 (A and B), S044 (A only)) and 4 mixed-or-none labels on a leaning prompt (S021 (A only), S045 (A only), S053 (A and B), S058 (A and B)); 3 of the flagged texts were labelled by A only and none by B only (`stance_vs_prompt_wording.csv`, `stance_prompt_direction_summary.csv`, `stance_prompt_direction_flags.csv`). Codebook rule 5 says to label what the text says, so a difference from the prompt direction is not an error in itself, and B's perfect match is consistent with answers that really argue the prompted side but cannot be told apart from labelling the prompt. The list is used only to choose texts to discuss; no label has been chosen or changed from it.
- **Adjudication (the labellers' joint decision; final version imported 2026-10-31).** The two labellers settled the 11 disputed shared texts and re-examined 5 single-labeller texts, and wrote a consensus label for 16 of the 16 (`stance_adjudication_outcome.csv`: ids, labels and reasons, no text). Disputed texts: the consensus equals labeller B's label in 10, labeller A's in 0, and is a label neither labeller had given in 1 (S029 mixed). Single-label texts: 2 changed (S033 mixed → none, S044 for → mixed) and 3 confirmed (S021 none, S045 none, S059 mixed). **A reason is recorded for only 1 of the 16 texts**, so the adjudication is documented by its labels, not by its reasoning. The adjudicated labels are agreed by the people who produced them, so they are not an independent check on the labellers, and the agreement figures above stay the measure of reliability. The reference has n = 60: chatbot (30) for 14, against 7, mixed 7, none 2; news (30) for 1, against 0, mixed 3, none 26; the only news text labelled for or against is S052 (for).
- **Stance baseline (VADER, TF-IDF) against the adjudicated reference (n = 60), stratified cross-validation over 20 seeds** (`stance_baseline_three_references.csv`, mean kappa and [min, max] over seeds). All texts: majority class +0.000, VADER features +0.190 [+0.095, +0.283], TF-IDF + logistic regression +0.500 [+0.462, +0.543]; accuracy 0.467, 0.422 and 0.672. **Most of that above-chance score is the chatbot-versus-news difference, not stance.** Within chatbot texts (n = 30) TF-IDF reaches only +0.147 [+0.023, +0.227] (slight) and VADER +0.014 [-0.131, +0.118]; within news texts (n = 30) TF-IDF +0.000 (it predicts none for every article: 26 of 30 news labels are none) and VADER +0.017 [-0.104, +0.091]. So within a source the baselines recover little or no stance; with only 1 news text labelled for or against, the news-only rows say little. (The 'majority class' row predicts the overall majority, none, so it scores 0.067 accuracy on chatbot texts.) Against each labeller's own 40 labels the result is labeller-dependent: VADER on all texts +0.026 for A and +0.352 for B, and within chatbot texts -0.107 for A and +0.276 for B, which is consistent with the weak human agreement (`stance_baseline_three_references.csv`).
- **Stance baseline before adjudication (earlier reference, kept for comparison).** Reference = agreed shared labels plus single-labeller labels, disputed texts excluded (n = 49): kappa majority class 0.000, VADER 0.041, TF-IDF 0.363 (`stance_baseline.csv`). It is superseded by the adjudicated reference above, on which the all-texts kappa is higher for VADER and higher for TF-IDF.
- **Stance is confounded with source in both labellers' labels** (`stance_labels_by_source_and_labeller.csv`). Labeller A: news: for 0, against 0, mixed 7, none 13; chatbot: for 8, against 5, mixed 5, none 2. Labeller B: news: for 1, against 0, mixed 1, none 18; chatbot: for 11, against 4, mixed 5, none 0. News texts are almost all none or mixed and the for/against labels sit on chatbot answers, so a classifier can score above the majority class just by recognising the source; the baseline kappas above partly measure that.

## Other limits that matter for reading this page

- Full-text news is about four times longer than a chatbot answer (median 928 against 259 words). Rarefaction fixes the estimator's dependence on hit count, not real diversity: a longer article can genuinely cover more frames, which works against H1.
- 18 question clusters, two small local models, a corpus of 420 articles (US 133, Other 104, UK 101, EU 82) selected by an AI word in the URL, and a lexicon edited (v1.2) after hit counts had been seen, before any H test. See `docs/limitations.md`.
- **The validation sheet shows excerpts, and the dataset's labels agree less on them.** The 50-text sheet (in the git-ignored `data/`) holds the first 300 words of 25 chatbot answers and a topic-selected 250 to 300 word window of 25 news articles (`validation_news_windows.csv`; 23 of 25 windows pass the on-topic rule, the other 2 articles have only two regulation words, far apart). Against the dataset's own labels, on the 19 sheet articles that have one, the dictionary agrees in 58% of cases on the full text (kappa 0.43) but 37% on the window shown to labellers (kappa 0.15), and the window's top frame equals the full-text top frame for 17 of 25 articles (`dataset_label_comparison_windows_summary.csv`). With n = 19 these are indications only; they say the human validation above tests the dictionary on excerpts that do not always carry the full-text label.
