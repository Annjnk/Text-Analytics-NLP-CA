# Limitations (ready to paste; edit to the report's voice)

*Figures are read from the repository's result files by `10_limitations_and_ethics.ipynb`.*

**1. Two small local models at one temperature.** The chatbot side of the study uses two open-weight models run locally through Ollama: Llama 3.1 8B (4-bit quantised, Q4_K_M) and Qwen3.5 9B (run on Apple's MLX runtime in 4-bit NVFP4, with its reasoning mode switched off so that answers are plain prose). Both were sampled at temperature 0.7 and top-p 0.95, with the replicate number as the random seed and two answers per prompt, giving 216 answers to 54 prompts that each ask for "about 250 words of plain prose, without headings or bullet points". Our results therefore describe these two models under these settings, not "AI chatbots" in general: larger hosted models, other temperatures, system prompts, or users who ask follow-up questions may produce different frame mixes. Quantisation can also change outputs slightly. The two models differ in length (mean 223 words for Llama and 295 for Qwen against the requested 250), which is why H1 compares texts at an equal number of frame hits (rarefaction) and why length-matched windows and fixed 300- and 150-word cuts are robustness checks. The two models also behave differently on H1 (see item 7), so "the chatbots" is not one thing.

**2. Gemini was dropped.** We first collected answers from Google's `gemini-3.8-flash` through the free tier of Google AI Studio, but the free quota (20 requests per day for that model) was exhausted after 21 answers (4 in a smoke test and 17 in the full run). We replaced it with Qwen3.5 9B, which also let us keep the design of two chatbots and two replicates. The 21 partial Gemini answers were removed from the analysis file. `dataset/answers/run_metadata.json` still lists the two Gemini attempts; they are **annotated as abandoned** (not deleted), so the record of what was run is complete. The consequence is that no large hosted commercial model is included; the plan already named the second chatbot as the first item to cut under time pressure.

**3. The AI-word URL filter skews the corpus towards AI-focused coverage.** To get enough on-topic articles from a 1.1-million-row URL list that has no article text, we kept only URLs with an AI term in the path (for example `ai`, `chatgpt`, `openai`, `chatbot`): 37,832 of 314,876 URLs in the July 2025 to June 2026 window. An earlier attempt without this filter (900 URLs, April to June 2026, three regions) gave only 45 usable articles; with the filter the pilot on-topic rate was 22%. The price is selection bias: articles in which AI is a side topic, and outlets that do not put AI in their URLs, are missing, so the frame distributions describe AI-focused coverage, not all news that mentions AI regulation. The filter is applied identically to every region, so regional comparisons are not distorted by it, but absolute frame emphasis may be. EU and Other were also restricted to likely-English domains, each outlet was capped at 15 articles, and the window is twelve months, not the dataset's full 2022 to 2026 span.

**4. Sample sizes are modest and uneven.** The frozen corpus has 420 articles from 285 outlets (US 133, Other 104, UK 101, EU 82); the largest outlet contributes 11 articles. The EU and UK strata are the smallest (82 and 101), so every EU and UK interval is wide and the region comparison (H3, exploratory by design) is weakest exactly where policy interest is highest. Duplicates matter too: the exact-duplicate rule removed 84 of 504 on-topic articles (17%; 83 of the 84 were copies of the same story on a different outlet, one on a sister site of the same publisher), but 14 clusters of near-duplicate articles (33 articles; 19 redundant) remain in the frozen corpus, concentrated in a few news events, so articles are not fully independent. "Other" means English-language outlets outside the US, EU and UK and says nothing about the rest of the world. Intervals therefore use a cluster bootstrap (outlet for news, question for chatbots), and chatbot intervals rest on only 18 question clusters.

**5. Few frame hits, and how the comparison handles it.** Frame entropy needs enough hits to be meaningful. Scored in full, 7 of 420 news articles (1.7%) have fewer than 5 hits (none of the chatbot answers do); in the fixed cuts the share grows: 24% of articles are unscorable at 300 words and 60% at 150 words, and 17% to 24% of the topic-selected windows have fewer than 5 hits. A plain entropy comparison would be biased twice: texts with few hits have a lower entropy by arithmetic, and dropping them would keep only the most frame-dense articles. We therefore (i) score every text in full (decision of 2026-10-29) and compare groups on rarefied entropy, drawing 5 hits at random without replacement 1,000 times per text, using only texts with at least 5 hits, (ii) report the share of texts under 5 hits as a result in its own right (frame density), (iii) bound the effect of the excluded texts by scoring them at the minimum, at the group median and at the maximum possible entropy, (iv) repeat at 3 and 8 hits, (v) run a pooled version that sums the hits of all texts in a group so that every text is used, and (vi) repeat the comparison on topic-selected windows matched to the answer lengths and on fixed 300- and 150-word cuts. A related limit is that the on-topic rule was applied to the whole article: only 157 of 420 corpus articles (37%) still satisfy it within their first 300 words, so the fixed-cut checks and the 300-word chunks of the validation sheet see only part of the regulatory content of an article.

**6. News is much longer than the answers, and rarefaction does not fix that.** Scored whole, articles have a median of 928 words against 259 for chatbot answers. Rarefaction removes the dependence of entropy on the number of hits, which is the part of length that biases the estimator, but not real diversity: a longer article can genuinely cover more frames, which raises news entropy and so works against H1. After rarefaction entropy is nearly uncorrelated with word count and hit count within each model (`entropy_length_correlations_full_text.csv`); the pooled-chatbot correlations come from pooling two models that differ in both length and entropy.

**7. The H1 result depends on the model and on analysis choices.** The main difference in mean rarefied entropy (chatbot minus news) is -0.004 [-0.035, +0.027] for Llama, +0.058 [+0.037, +0.078] for Qwen and +0.027 [+0.002, +0.051] for the pooled chatbots. Across 14 robustness runs Qwen's interval excludes zero with chatbot higher in 13, the pooled chatbots' in 9 and Llama's in 2; the pooled-counts run shows no difference for any comparison. So H1 is supported for one of the two models, the pooled claim is marginal and fragile, and the gaps are small (a few hundredths on a scale whose maximum at K = 5 is 0.898). Chatbot intervals rest on only 18 question clusters. H2 (leaning prompts) and H3 (regions) found no effect and no reliably closer region; with 18 questions and 82 to 133 articles per region that is an absence of evidence, not evidence of absence.

## Further limits found while checking the work

- **The dictionary measures words, not meaning.** It cannot tell "regulation protects people" from "regulation burdens firms"; frames are emphasis, not stance. The lexicon was edited twice, both times before any hypothesis test: v1.1 after an audit of hit counts on the chatbot answers, and v1.2 (2026-10-27, signed off) after hit counts in both corpora had been seen (9 terms with no hits dropped, 7 added); the changes are in `dataset/frames/CHANGELOG.md`, and the lexicon-variant runs (no governance frame, no ambiguous terms, prompt-echo terms, no "lawsuit") check that no H1 result rests on those choices. Topic-defining words (regulate, law, rule, government, policy) are left out on purpose because they occur in almost every text.
- **Our dictionary and the dataset's own frame labels disagree.** On the 304 corpus articles where the dataset has a dominant frame, our top frame (full-text scoring) agrees with it in 42% of cases (Cohen's kappa 0.27). The two instruments read different material (the dataset uses GDELT entity names and quotations, we use article text), so neither is the truth. Human validation is the real test and has **not been done**: nobody has labelled the 50-text sheet (25 chatbot answers and 25 news articles, drawn at random within groups without using the dictionary). The sheet shows excerpts, not the full texts the main analysis scores: the first 300 words of each answer and, for each article, a topic-selected window of 254 to 299 words (23 of 25 windows pass the on-topic rule; the other 2 articles have only two regulation words, far apart). On the 19 sheet articles that have a dataset label, the dictionary agrees with the dataset in 58% of cases on the full text but 37% on the window, and the window's top frame equals the full-text top frame for only 68% of the 25 articles, so the sheet tests the dictionary on excerpts that do not always carry the full-text label (small numbers: indications only).
- **Style differs far more than framing.** A classifier using only function words, punctuation and formatting separates chatbot answers from news with an AUC of 1.00 on held-out outlets and questions; a classifier using only the six frame proportions reaches 0.70, and 0.72 to 0.74 on a chatbot it never saw. Formatting features (paragraph breaks, digits) do not overlap between the two sources, so a matched subset cannot remove them; entropy differences must be read with this in mind.
- **Text quality.** Scraped text contains some boilerplate, leftover markup and encoding errors (4% of chunks carry subscription or sign-up text), which dilutes frame density slightly.
- **Stance labelling and actors.** Stance labels rest on two labellers and 20 shared texts, so kappa has a wide interval. Named-entity recognition uses a small general model and an alias table that covers the most frequent actors only; chatbot answers name almost no actors.
- **The frame-composition finding is exploratory, and governance is not clean.** Chatbots put more of their hits in innovation/opportunity (+0.069 against news, pooled) and governance (+0.060) and fewer in economy/labour (-0.114); that is 12 comparisons without correction. Governance is partly present by design (every prompt asks what governments should do, and 3 of the 18 questions are about governance), and the on-topic filter that built the news corpus counts words that are also governance lexicon terms (regulator, regulatory, oversight, legislation, governance, lawmaker, ...), so part of the news governance share is built in by selection. Removing those 9 terms from both groups lowers the news governance share (0.227 to 0.161) and **raises** the pooled chatbot-minus-news governance difference from +0.060 to +0.079 (Llama +0.034 to +0.065, Qwen +0.087 to +0.093), so the overlap masks the difference rather than creating it; the prompt-design caveat is unaffected. See `dataset/analysis/RESULTS.md`.
