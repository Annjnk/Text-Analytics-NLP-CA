# Limitations (ready to paste; edit to the report's voice)

*Figures are read from the repository's result files by `10_limitations_and_ethics.ipynb`.*

**1. Two small local models at one temperature.** The chatbot side of the study uses two open-weight models run locally through Ollama: Llama 3.1 8B (4-bit quantised, Q4_K_M) and Qwen3.5 9B (run on Apple's MLX runtime in 4-bit NVFP4, with its reasoning mode switched off so that answers are plain prose). Both were sampled at temperature 0.7 and top-p 0.95, with the replicate number as the random seed and two answers per prompt, giving 216 answers to 54 prompts that each ask for "about 250 words of plain prose, without headings or bullet points". Our results therefore describe these two models under these settings, not "AI chatbots" in general: larger hosted models, other temperatures, system prompts, or users who ask follow-up questions may produce different frame mixes. Quantisation can also change outputs slightly. The two models differ in length (mean 223 words for Llama and 287 for Qwen against the requested 250), which is why every comparison is made on texts cut to 300 words and at a matched number of frame hits.

**2. Gemini was dropped.** We first collected answers from Google's `gemini-3.8-flash` through the free tier of Google AI Studio, but the free quota (20 requests per day for that model) was exhausted after 21 answers (4 in a smoke test and 17 in the full run). We replaced it with Qwen3.5 9B, which also let us keep the design of two chatbots and two replicates. The 21 partial Gemini answers were removed from the analysis file. `dataset/answers/run_metadata.json` still lists the two Gemini attempts; they are **annotated as abandoned** (not deleted), so the record of what was run is complete. The consequence is that no large hosted commercial model is included; the plan already named the second chatbot as the first item to cut under time pressure.

**3. The AI-word URL filter skews the corpus towards AI-focused coverage.** To get enough on-topic articles from a 1.1-million-row URL list that has no article text, we kept only URLs with an AI term in the path (for example `ai`, `chatgpt`, `openai`, `chatbot`): 37,832 of 314,876 URLs in the July 2025 to June 2026 window. An earlier attempt without this filter (900 URLs, April to June 2026, three regions) gave only 45 usable articles; with the filter the pilot on-topic rate was 22%. The price is selection bias: articles in which AI is a side topic, and outlets that do not put AI in their URLs, are missing, so the frame distributions describe AI-focused coverage, not all news that mentions AI regulation. The filter is applied identically to every region, so regional comparisons are not distorted by it, but absolute frame emphasis may be. EU and Other were also restricted to likely-English domains, each outlet was capped at 15 articles, and the window is twelve months, not the dataset's full 2022 to 2026 span.

**4. Sample sizes are modest and uneven.** The frozen corpus has 420 articles from 285 outlets (US 133, Other 104, UK 101, EU 82); the largest outlet contributes 11 articles. The EU and UK strata are the smallest (82 and 101), so every EU and UK interval is wide and the region comparison (H3, exploratory by design) is weakest exactly where policy interest is highest. Duplicates matter too: the exact-duplicate rule removed 84 of 504 on-topic articles (17%; 83 of the 84 were copies of the same story on a different outlet, one on a sister site of the same publisher), but 14 clusters of near-duplicate articles (33 articles; 19 redundant) remain in the frozen corpus, concentrated in a few news events, so articles are not fully independent. "Other" means English-language outlets outside the US, EU and UK and says nothing about the rest of the world. Intervals therefore use a cluster bootstrap (outlet for news, question for chatbots), and chatbot intervals rest on only 18 question clusters.

**5. A quarter of news chunks have too few frame hits to score, and how we handle it.** Frame entropy needs enough hits to be meaningful. At the 300-word cut 108 of 420 news chunks (26%) have fewer than 5 hits (none of the chatbot answers do), and the share rises to 39% when news is cut to Llama's shorter answer lengths and 27% for Qwen's. A plain entropy comparison would be biased twice: texts with few hits have a lower entropy by arithmetic, and dropping them would keep only the most frame-dense articles. We therefore (i) report the share of texts under 5 hits as a result in its own right (frame density), (ii) compare groups on rarefied entropy, drawing 5 hits at random without replacement 1,000 times per text, using only texts with at least 5 hits, (iii) bound the effect of the excluded texts by scoring them at the minimum, at the group median and at the maximum possible entropy, (iv) repeat at 3 and 8 hits, and (v) run a pooled version that sums the hits of all texts in a group so that every text is used. A related limit is that the on-topic rule was applied to the whole article while frames are scored on its first 300 words: only 157 of 420 corpus articles (37%) still satisfy the on-topic rule within those 300 words, so much of the regulatory content of an article sits beyond the part that is scored.

## Further limits found while checking the work

- **The dictionary measures words, not meaning.** It cannot tell "regulation protects people" from "regulation burdens firms"; frames are emphasis, not stance. The lexicon was refined once, after an audit of hit counts on the chatbot answers and before any hypothesis test; the changes are in `dataset/frames/CHANGELOG.md`. Topic-defining words (regulate, law, rule, government, policy) are left out on purpose because they occur in almost every text.
- **Our dictionary and the dataset's own frame labels disagree.** On the 304 corpus articles where the dataset has a dominant frame, our top frame agrees with it in 35% of cases (Cohen's kappa 0.20). The two instruments read different material (the dataset uses GDELT entity names and quotations, we use article text), so neither is the truth; human validation of 50 chunks is the real test and is still to be done by the team.
- **Style differs far more than framing.** A classifier using only function words, punctuation and formatting separates chatbot answers from news with an AUC of 1.00 on held-out outlets and questions; a classifier using only the six frame proportions reaches 0.68. Formatting features (paragraph breaks, digits) do not overlap between the two sources, so a matched subset cannot remove them; entropy differences must be read with this in mind.
- **Text quality.** Scraped text contains some boilerplate, leftover markup and encoding errors (4% of chunks carry subscription or sign-up text), which dilutes frame density slightly.
- **Stance labelling and actors.** Stance labels rest on two labellers and 20 shared texts, so kappa has a wide interval. Named-entity recognition uses a small general model and an alias table that covers the most frequent actors only; chatbot answers name almost no actors.
